## Context

The app is now multi-user and gamified. Those two changes together create a risk that did not
exist when the only user was the author: **a reward system that shapes the training behaviour of
people we will never meet.**

The default pattern in fitness apps is to reward *more* — more sessions, more kilometres, more
consecutive days. It works, in the sense that it increases engagement. It also produces the
industry's characteristic harm: people training through illness to protect a streak, adding
volume they were not prepared for, and getting injured.

This product has a specific and unusual property that makes the harm sharper: **it prescribes
rest.** The planner schedules rest days and deload cycles ([01 §3.3](../01-business-requirements.md)).
A conventional daily streak would therefore have the app rewarding users for disobeying its own
programme. That is not merely bad design, it is incoherent — and the incoherence would be resolved
by users in the direction of more training, because that is what the points said to do.

The same property is also an opportunity nobody else has. Because the app knows what was
*prescribed*, it can reward following a plan rather than maximising output. A step counter cannot
tell a rest day from a lazy day. This app can.

## Options considered

**A. Conventional volume/streak gamification.** Daily streaks, XP per kilometre and per set,
leaderboards. Proven engagement mechanics, familiar to users, cheap to build.
Rejected: it directly contradicts the planner, and its known failure mode is injury.

**B. No gamification.** Zero risk of shaping behaviour badly. Rejected — the user asked for it,
and adherence is genuinely the hardest problem in training. A system that helps people finish
blocks is doing real good.

**C. Reward adherence to the prescription, including rest.** Points for doing what the plan said.
Full credit for a deload cycle completed as prescribed. Nothing for exceeding a target.

**D. Reward measured outcomes only** (e1RM gains, pace improvements). Honest, but outcomes are
lagging, noisy, and plateau — a beginner would be showered with rewards and an advanced trainee
starved, which is backwards, since the advanced trainee's discipline is the greater achievement.

## Decision

**Option C**, with option D's outcome measures as one track (Progression) among the others
([08 §2](../08-gamification.md)).

Two invariants follow, and they are safety properties rather than preferences:

- **INV-21** — XP is awarded exactly once per logical event, by a pure function of prescription
  and log.
- **INV-22** — No reward is ever given for exceeding a prescription, and none is withheld for
  rest. Nothing decays, expires, or is deducted.

> **Terminology correction (2026-09-08).** This ADR was drafted saying "week" throughout. The
> repeating unit is the **microcycle** of user-chosen length (INV-25), and for the streak mechanic
> that distinction is load-bearing rather than cosmetic: a streak counted in calendar weeks is
> simply wrong for anyone running a 9-day cycle, and it would reintroduce exactly the
> calendar-anxiety this ADR exists to remove. The argument below is unchanged; the unit is the
> microcycle. See [08 §5](../08-gamification.md).

Concretely, the value system as expressed in numbers ([08 §3](../08-gamification.md)):

| | |
|---|---|
| Deload cycle completed as prescribed | **300 XP** |
| Ordinary prescribed session | 100 XP |
| Per-cycle volume exceeded by > 25 % | **0 XP** |
| Prescribed rest day respected | 25 XP |

A deload cycle is worth three sessions. Overreaching is worth nothing. Streaks are counted in
**microcycles**, tolerate a cycle whose prescription was rest or deload, and carry one free cycle
per quarter so that nobody has an incentive to train while ill.

**No public leaderboards in v1.** Comparative ranking rewards whoever trained most, which is the
exact behaviour this ADR exists to discourage, with social pressure attached. If comparison
arrives in v2 it compares **adherence to each person's own plan**, in private groups — two people
each hitting 100 % of different programmes are equals.

## Consequences

**Good**
- The reward system and the planner say the same thing. A user optimising for points is training
  well, by construction.
- "You took your deload — that is the hard part" is a genuinely novel thing for an app to say,
  and it is only sayable because the app knows the prescription.
- Removes the injury-pressure mechanics wholesale rather than trying to tune them.
- No loss aversion means no anxiety-driven engagement, and no engagement metric that looks good
  while harming people.

**Bad — accepted**
- **Lower engagement than option A.** Daily streaks and loss aversion work; that is why they are
  everywhere. This will retain fewer users, and that is the price. If retention later becomes a
  business pressure, the response is better training outcomes, not this ADR being quietly relaxed.
- Unplanned training is worth less than planned training, which will feel unfair to users who
  train intuitively. Mitigated by still awarding it, at a reduced rate — never zero.
- The scorer needs the prescription as an input, so it is coupled to the planner and cannot be
  built as an isolated "points service".
- Harder to explain in a store listing than "earn points, keep your streak".

## Revisit if

- Evidence emerges that users are gaming the adherence system by writing themselves trivially
  easy plans. The likely answer is to weight XP by a plan's own progression, **not** to reintroduce
  volume rewards.
- Private-group comparison ships in v2 — that needs its own ADR, and it must not become a
  leaderboard by another name.
- **The monetisation model ever moves away from a flat subscription.** Advertising, consumable
  IAP, or any engagement-metered revenue would put this ADR in direct conflict with the P&L, and
  invariants that conflict with revenue lose eventually. That change would require re-deciding
  this ADR from scratch, not amending it. See [ADR-006](ADR-006.md).
